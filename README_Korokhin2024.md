# Reproducing Korokhin et al. (2024): mapping lunar soil composition with M³ and machine learning

**Paper:** V. Korokhin, Y. Surkov, U. Mall et al. (2024), *Applying machine learning to a nonlinear
spectral mixing model for mapping lunar soils composition using Chandrayaan-1 M³ data*,
Planetary and Space Science 244, 105870. doi:10.1016/j.pss.2024.105870

**Code and full results:** folder `Korokhin2024_ShkuratovANN/`

---

## 1. Aim

To rebuild the paper's method from scratch, run it on the same data, and check whether its
published numbers and claims hold. Every published number we could check was written down, with an
allowed tolerance, **before** our results existed, and then scored automatically.

## 2. What the paper does

It estimates, for every pixel of an M³ image, what the lunar soil is made of. It does this in
three steps:

1. **A physical model** (Shkuratov et al., 1999) predicts the reflectance spectrum of a soil from
   eight properties:
   - grain size;
   - space-weathering iron (nanophase iron);
   - a dark, glassy "gray" component;
   - five minerals: clinopyroxene, orthopyroxene, olivine, plagioclase and ilmenite.
2. **The model is run backwards**, to find the eight values that best reproduce each measured
   spectrum. The paper does this in two ways:
   - **Careful fitting**, pixel by pixel (Nelder–Mead optimizer). This is accurate but slow.
   - **A neural network** trained on spectra generated by the model. This is fast. Its size is
     21 inputs → 42 → 12 → 8 outputs, and it is trained in three rounds.
3. **The maps are compared** with maps made by older, published methods.

## 4. How the checking was done

- The model, the image cleaning, the fitting and the network were all implemented as the paper
  describes them.
- **Where the paper leaves something open, the choice is recorded** (22 in total), and where it
  matters, the alternatives are run side by side.
- **Scorecard:** 65 of the paper's numbers and claims are scored against tolerances fixed in
  advance. Another 91 values are reported for context.
- **Extra tests the paper did not run:**
  - Can the network recover the right answer on spectra where the answer is known?
  - Do the maps change if the network's training settings change?
  - Does the method get the right mineral content for real lunar soils of known composition?
- The whole pipeline was re-run from empty folders and gave identical results.

## 5. Outcome

**Of the 65 scored items:**
- **46 pass;**
- **6 miss narrowly** (within 1.5 × the tolerance);
- **13 miss clearly.**

**What reproduces:**
- the physical model, exactly;
- the laboratory mixture tests, once a misprint in the paper is corrected (see below);
- the network's accuracy after its second and third training rounds, and its values at the
  highland test sites;
- grain size and space weathering: grain size rises with sieve size in 16 of 18 lab soils, and
  weathering follows the measured maturity;
- the speed claim: the network maps the image in under a second; careful fitting would take
  about 550 CPU-hours.

**Main findings:**
1. **Three of the eight maps come from the training settings, not from the image.** On spectra
   with known answers, the network cannot recover orthopyroxene, olivine or the gray component,
   and it does little better than always guessing the middle of its training range. Shifting
   that range shifts these maps almost one-for-one. The paper's maps for them sit exactly at the
   middle of the range.
2. **One mineral is mislabelled.** The spectrum used as "orthopyroxene" is a pigeonite, according
   to its own RELAB label.
3. **The paper's Table 1 has two columns swapped** (olivine and pyroxene). RELAB's own label for
   one of the mixtures confirms it.
4. **Where the network and careful fitting disagree, careful fitting fits the spectra better.**
   For clinopyroxene, the two even move in opposite directions.
5. **The "ambiguity" the network is said to remove disappears** when the fitting is done as
   thoroughly as the paper describes.
6. **On 19 real lunar soils, the mineral amounts are mostly no better than a blind guess.** The
   guess used for comparison is "this soil is like the average of the others". The method tells
   highland soils from mare soils, but not the amounts within either.

## 6. Why some results do not match

| what does not match | paper | ours | reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lab mixtures, olivine / pyroxene (4 clear misses, 1 near miss) | — | off by 6–17 points | Misprint in the paper: the two columns are swapped. Read the other way round, all eight values agree within 2.5 points. |
| Comparisons with older methods (e.g. plagioclase 0.46 vs 0.92; maturity 0.61 vs 0.85; titanium 0.66 vs 0.91) | high correlations | lower | Not caused by our image processing: switching between two different cleanings changed these by at most 0.1. The older titanium network's published weights cannot reproduce even its own paper's results, so it was retrained from that paper's data. |
| Calibration equation (Eq. 2): the offset | 0.036 | 0.016 | The slope matches (1.669 vs 1.685). No lab sample or processing variant gives the paper's offset, and the paper does not give enough detail to trace it. |
| Ilmenite in the dark glassy soil sample | 3.2 % | 9.2 % | Not explained. The other eight values of the same fit match. |
| Test-site fits | — | — | Site 1 matches closely. Site 2 fits slightly worse than printed (1.07 % vs 0.70 % error). The site positions had to be reconstructed from a figure. |
| Network error after the first training round | 2.39 % | 3.07 % | Close to the tolerance (a near miss). The later rounds match. In the paper those rounds trained for over 4,000 cycles; ours stop improving after about 100, and longer training does not help. |
| Glassy sample used as a building block: totals above 110 % at every site | yes | 3 of 4 sites | Site 3 comes to 105 %. The sites are reconstructed from a figure. |
| Table 10 "several equally good answers" | yes | no | A thorough search always finds the same, single best answer. |

**Would the authors' own processed image change this?** Probably not. The paper says its data are
"available on request". But replacing a rough image cleaning with the authors' exact method moved
the comparisons by at most 0.1, much less than the gaps above, and the main findings (1–5) came
out the same under both cleanings.

## 7. Bottom line

The method works and is very fast, and it captures grain size and space weathering. But three of
its eight mineral maps (orthopyroxene, olivine and the gray component) reflect the settings chosen
before training, not the data. Where the network and direct fitting disagree, direct fitting is
the more trustworthy. The published comparisons with older methods do not reproduce at the
strength reported.

**Where to look next:** in the folder, `RESULTS.md` has the full scorecard, `PAPER_NOTES.md`
records every choice and finding with its evidence, and `SOURCES_AND_LIMITATIONS.md` lists the sources and
limitations.

## In short

We rebuilt the paper's method and ran it on the same Chandrayaan-1 M³ image of Shapley crater
(M3G20090728T214215). 46 of the paper's 65 checkable results match, 6 more are very close, and 13
do not. The physics model, the lab tests and the speed of the neural network all hold up. But for
three of the eight things it maps (orthopyroxene, olivine and the gray glassy component), the
network cannot read the amount from the spectrum. It returns roughly the middle of the range the
authors chose before training, so these three maps show that starting assumption, not the Moon's
actual composition. The paper also has a few errors: two table columns swapped, and one mineral
mislabelled. Its comparisons with older methods are weaker than reported. The code, data and full
results are in the `Korokhin2024_ShkuratovANN` folder.

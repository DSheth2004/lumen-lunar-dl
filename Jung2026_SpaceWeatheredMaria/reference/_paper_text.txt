Abstract
Visible-to-shortwave infrared (VSWIR) reflectance spectroscopy has revolutionized our understanding of
planetary surface compositions. However, space-weathering processes on airless bodies complicate quantitative
compositional analyses. Here, we present a framework to isolate the signatures of space weathering in VSWIR
spectra of lunar maria by leveraging radiative transfer modeling under the assumptions that (i) a space-weathered
target can be expressed as a mixture of fresh and fully space-weathered components and (ii) remaining signatures
can be modeled by including agglutinates as an end-member component. We first validate this approach against
laboratory spectra of space-weathered Apollo mare soils of known mineral compositions using a probabilistic
Markov Chain Monte Carlo implementation of the Hapke radiative transfer model. Second, we illustrate how this
approach can be applied to orbital Moon Mineralogy Mapper data. The proposed space-weathering correction
workflow for lunar maria could be expanded to other lunar lithologies and applied to existing and future data sets.

1. Introduction
Applications of visible-to-shortwave infrared (VSWIR)
reflectance spectroscopy in multiband and hyperspectral data
sets have provided abundant information on the compositions of
planetary surfaces, including interpretations of primary and
secondary mineralogy. Numerous studies have utilized VSWIR
spectroscopy for basic qualitative identification of various
minerals based on their distinctive electronic and vibrational
absorptions, including for the Moon, Mars, Ceres, and near-
Earth asteroids (C. M. Pieters 1986; J.-P. Bibring et al. 2005;
R. E. Arvidson et al. 2014; B. L. Berg et al. 2016; D. C. Reuter
et al. 2018; L. Riu et al. 2021). Less commonly, some studies
have attempted to quantify the abundance of various mineral
phases on planetary bodies using different radiative transfer
models (RTMs; B. Hapke 1981; Y. Shkuratov et al. 1999;
S. J. Lawrence & P. G. Lucey 2007; F. Poulet et al. 2008).
Under classic RTM approaches, a single set of minerals is
inferred from the best-fit modeled spectrum to the data
(J. T. Cahill & P. G. Lucey 2007; B. W. Denevi et al. 2008;
J. T. S. Cahill et al. 2010; S. Li & L. Li 2011), which might not
be representative of actual mineralogy due to the non-unique
nature of the inverse problem (M. G. A. Lapôtre et al. 2017a). In
contrast, probabilistic implementations of such models recog-
nize the non-uniqueness of spectral unmixing and instead output
ranges of possible mineral assemblages (M. G. A. Lapôtre et al.
2017a, 2017b; H. Kurokawa et al. 2020; I. Mishra et al. 2021;
C. Pilorget & J. Fernando 2021; O. King et al. 2022). Other
approaches to modeling VSWIR reflectance spectra include the
modified Gaussian method (J. M. Sunshine et al. 1990;
J. M. Sunshine & C. M. Pieters 1993; S. K. Noble et al.
2006; H. Clénet et al. 2011; P. J. Isaacson et al. 2011) and
shallow machine learning techniques (S. Li et al. 2012;
M. Hareyama et al. 2019; G. R. Kodikara & L. J. McHenry
2020). Retrieving quantitative estimates of mineralogy from
reflectance spectra of planetary surfaces that have been
significantly modified by space weathering—such as the
Moon—has received even less attention.
Building on early telescopic observations, reflectance data
acquired from lunar orbit by the Clementine and Lunar
Prospector missions facilitated the identification of spectrally
and presumably mineralogically distinct regions at ultraviolet-
to-visible wavelengths (B. L. Jolliff et al. 2000; C. M. Pieters
et al. 2001; P. G. Lucey 2004). More recently, the Kaguya
Multiband Imager (9 bands; M. Ohtake et al. 2008), Kaguya
Spectral Profiler (296 spectral channels; T. Matsunaga et al.
2008), and Chandryaan1 Moon Mineralogy Mapper (M3; 86
spectral channels in global mode and 260 in targeted mode;
J. N. Goswami & M. Annadurai 2008; C. M. Pieters et al.
2009; R. O. Green et al. 2011) instruments have allowed
scientists to map the distribution of water, constrain volcanic
stratigraphy, and detect small outcrops of olivine-rich,
Fe-plagioclase-rich, and spinel-bearing zones (C. M. Pieters
et al. 2009; C. Pieters et al. 2014; M. Hareyama et al. 2019;
M. Lemelin et al. 2019).
However, quantifying mineral abundances on the Moon has
remained challenging due to the strong spectral modifications
imparted by space-weathering processes (L. A. Taylor et al.
2000; D. Trang & P. G. Lucey 2019; Figure 1). These include
micrometeorite bombardment and interactions with solar
winds and result in the formation of (1) impact-vitrified glass
in agglutinates (aggregates of soil particles; B. Hapke 2001;
C. N. Yasanayake et al. 2024) and (2) nanophase (and
microphase) metallic iron (npFe
0
) on the rims (or inside) of
mineral grains (W. Cassidy & B. Hapke 1975; C. M. Pieters &
S. K. Noble 2016). These phenomena tend to decrease the
overall albedo, induce spectral reddening (a positive slope with

Many studies have attempted to parameterize the degree of
space weathering across the lunar surface or to use certain
magnetic properties to estimate the amount of npFe
0

space weathering, such as through spectral continuum removal

are not capable of removing or accounting for the spectral
signatures of space weathering in a way that allows for
quantitative estimates of modal mineralogy of lunar regolith.
One potential exception is the radiative transfer modeling of
B. Hapke (2001). Grounded in scattering theory, this approach
requires (a) the assignment of a preferred distribution of npFe
in the grains (e.g., homogenous throughout the grain versus
concentrated in rims) and (b) the treatment of the abundance
and size of npFe particles separately for each mineral phase in
the mixture, doubling the number of unknowns in the inverse
problem. Furthermore, the simultaneous presence of both
nanophase and microphase iron, which follow different
scattering regimes in the VSWIR range, is not taken into
account in the model of B. Hapke (2001), further challenging
its applicability to lunar regolith. Here, we present a new,
semiempirical framework to quantify modal mineralogy of
space-weathered regolith in lunar maria.
2. Approach
2.1. Step 1: Semiempirical Band Depth Correction
Using laboratory spectra of Apollo soil samples, we devised
a workflow to remove the spectral effects of space weathering
from VSWIR spectra of lunar materials (e.g., lunar regolith)
and estimate reflectance spectra of the “fresh” (unweathered)
material from which quantitative estimates of mineralogy can
be derived. We refer to the first step as a “band depth
correction” though it also affects spectral slopes. Qualitatively,
this step consists of scaling the band depth of a major
absorption in spectra affected by space weathering (specifi-
cally, by npFe
0
) to restore a fresh, unaltered appearance to the
spectrum, with corrected deeper absorption features.

2.2. Step 2: Spectral Modeling
The first step in the workflow yields a “depth-corrected”
spectrum, ω
dc
, which is primarily intended to account for the
influence of npFe
0
on absorption strength. However, it does
not fully account for how space-weathering influences spectral
slope, and it does not yet account for other spectral properties
associated with agglutinates, an important product of lunar
space weathering. Unlike components such as pyroxene,
olivine, or Fe-rich glass, agglutinates have almost no
absorption features and can be spectrally dominated by
npFe
0
(i.e., exhibit a red-sloped component in their spectrum).
The next step consists of estimating the residual signatures of
space weathering (including the red spectral slope) through
spectral unmixing of the intermediate, “depth-corrected”
spectrum, including agglutinates as a compositional end-
member, and ultimately removing them by forward modeling a
final corrected spectrum, ω
f
, by excluding the contribution of
the agglutinate component.
Due to the non-unique nature of spectral unmixing
inversions, we employed a Bayesian implementation of the
Hapke model, following M. G. A. Lapôtre et al. (2017a), to
unmix “depth-corrected” spectra (ω
dc
) and retrieve modal
estimates of dominant phases and corresponding particle sizes.
The inverse problem is solved using the cascading adaptive
transitional metropolis in parallel (CATMIP) algorithm
(S. E. Minson et al. 2013, 2014), a parallel Markov Chain
Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm (based on the Metropolis–
Hastings algorithm) that efficiently samples high-dimensional
spaces. This step provides quantitative estimates of modal
mineralogy from spectral data.
Prior distributions for mineral abundances were drawn from
Dirichlet distributions, ensuring that the sum of all abundances
is equal to 100%. The grain size of each end-member was
parameterized as a separate variable drawn from uniform prior
distributions, ranging from ∼0 to 990 μm in the initial setup
(Appendix B). Nine phases commonly found in lunar mare
basalts were selected as compositional end-members: olivine
(fayalitic olivine, Fo5-10), pigeonite, augite, plagioclase,
ilmenite, glass of pyroxene-like composition, glass of
plagioclase-like composition, Apollo orange glass, and
agglutinates (Appendix C; Figure C1; Table C1). Therefore,
a total of 18 parameters were considered. The glass with
pyroxene-like and plagioclase-like compositions refers to
synthetic amorphous glasses composed of pyroxene, plagio-
clase, and ilmenite in weight percentages of 66.7%/26.7%/
6.7% and 37.0%/59.3%/3.7%, respectively (E. A. Cloutis
et al. 1990). As this MCMC algorithm takes the optical
constants of each mineral as input (M. G. A. Lapôtre et al.
2017a), for each mineral end-member, we first converted its
reflectance spectra to SSA (Appendix D); the SSA values were
subsequently converted to optical constants (Appendix E).
The MCMC algorithm was run with a Markov chain length
of 10
6
and outputs posterior distributions for all 18 parameters
(a modal abundance vector and a grain size vector, each with
nine elements), permitting evaluation of the full suite of
mineral assemblages that yield a reasonable match to the data.
Acceptance and rejection of sampled compositional models are
decided based on the goodness of the fit between true and
modeled spectra. In addition to the full posterior distributions,
two specific models are considered: the maximum a posteriori,
or MAP (i.e., the model determined to have the overall highest
likelihood), estimation, and the mode (i.e., using the most
likely value for each of the 18 parameters, which does
not necessarily correspond to the MAP). From the various
possible model solutions, we use the resulting mineral
abundances and corresponding particle sizes to forward model
the spectrum of the fresh, non-space-weathered material,

3. Results and Interpretations
Application to Lunar Soils. Reflectance spectra of lunar soils
derived from lunar mare basalt regions with a range of soil
maturity (12001, I
sm

sm
= 14; 15071, I
sm

the method (Table 1). For all four LSCC mare basalt soil
samples examined here, the first step yields intermediate,
“depth-corrected” spectra with deeper absorptions, less spectral
reddening, and overall higher albedos (Figure 3( a)–(d)). The
rms values of accepted models for fitting the resulting ω
dc
spectra, including the mode and MAP, consistently exceed 0.98,
indicating that the depth-corrected spectra could be well
reproduced from linear mixtures of SSA spectra of the nine
selected compositional end-members (Figure 3( e)–(h)). Subse-
quent forward modeling without agglutinates yields corrected
spectra that resemble those of typical lunar basalts, with
pronounced 1 and 2 μm absorption bands and visibly less
spectral reddening (Figure 3(i)–(l)).
Though corrected LSCC spectra look qualitatively similar to
spectra of fresh lunar basalt, we further validated their
quantitative robustness by comparing inverted mineral abun-
dances with independently measured compositions or through
petrological analyses reported in the Apollo lunar compendium
(K. A. Cone et al. 2020). The 95% probability densities of
modeled abundances show substantial overlap with laboratory
measurements, demonstrating close agreement between mod-
eled and lab-measured mineral abundances (Figure 4). In some
cases, MAP estimates differ slightly from laboratory measure-
ments, emphasizing the need for probabilistic modeling rather
than relying on a single best-fit model. For olivine, differences
from LSCC are generally less than 7% relative to the mode and
the mean. Variability in pyroxene is expected, as different
pyroxene chemistries have been grouped into only two end-
members (pigeonite and augite), a limitation also noted by
J. T. S. Cahill et al. (2010). Although assigning a precise
degree of accuracy to these values remains challenging, we
aim to extend the analysis to a wider range of compositions
and chemistries in future tests to improve applicability. For
ilmenite, deviations are typically less than 10%, except for
sample 15071. In contrast, plagioclase exhibits larger dis-
crepancies, up to 20%–30% in some samples, reflecting the
inherent difficulty of resolving weak diagnostic absorption
features in plagioclase VSWIR spectra. In contrast with the
approach of B. Hapke (2001), our semiempirical framework
relies on lab-based measurements of true space-weathered
lunar samples, empirically circumventing the need to make
assumptions about the distribution of npFe (or microphase
iron). Furthermore, it allows us to keep the number of free
parameters to half of what would be required under the more

4. Discussion and Conclusions
We developed a framework to correct for the influence of
space weathering on VSWIR spectra of the lunar surface using
an empirical band depth correction and probabilistic unmixing
modeling. This approach was validated against laboratory
measurements of Apollo regolith samples and subsequently
applied to thermally corrected M3 reflectance data of the lunar
surface. The results demonstrate the potential of this workflow
to facilitate a computationally tractable, quantitative break-
down of mineralogy for remote compositional analyses on
the Moon.
In its current form, our semiempirical framework relies on
the assumption that the same band depth correction can be
applied broadly to mare basalts. In other words, it assumes that
different groups of mare basalts (e.g., illmenite-, olivine-, and
pyroxene-rich basalts) follow the same relationship between
their band depth at 970 nm and I
sm
(Equation (3)). As more
samples become available, the approach could be refined in
future iterations by incorporating different versions of
Equation (3) that are tailored to specific basalt types.
Due to the strongly nonlinear relationship between band
depths and soil maturity, our method is most effective for areas
with immature (I
sm
= 0–30) or submature soils (I
sm
= 30–60)
rather than mature soils (I
sm
= 60–100). Using Equation (3),
calculated Δ values for mature soils span a narrow range—
Δ = 0.98–1.00. In contrast, calculated Δ values in immature
and submature soils range from 0.00 to 0.98. In other words,
large changes in I
sm
in mature soils lead to very small changes
in band depth, such that band depth is a poor proxy for the
degree of space weathering in such samples. This approach,
moreover, relies on the availability of a space-weathered target
that possesses a mineral composition similar to that of the
target region. As a result, shallow young craters that expose
relatively fresh materials represent particularly promising
targets for this method. This technique could be adapted to
other lunar terrain types, such as lunar highlands (notably
through the use of different compositional end-members), and
possibly serve as a template for space-weathering corrections
and remote compositional analyses on other airless bodies.

Appendix B
MCMC Spectral Unmixing and Prior Distributions
Most spectral compositional analyses to date report a single
set of mineral abundances and grain sizes, corresponding
to the overall best-fit modeled spectrum (S. Li & L. Li 2011;
P. J. Isaacson et al. 2011). However, it is important to
recognize that the best-fit model does not necessarily reflect
the true composition, due to factors such as noise in the data or
slight variations in the chemistries of mineral end-members.
Furthermore, trade-offs between mineral abundances and grain
sizes create non-unique solutions to the spectral unmixing
problem, such that multiple solutions can explain the data
equally well (M. G. A. Lapôtre et al. 2017a). To address this
issue and provide a fuller picture of sets of possible
mineral assemblages and their likelihood, a probabilistic
approach was devised by M. G. A. Lapôtre et al. (2017a),
utilizing a Bayesian framework applied to the Hapke RTM.
This algorithm has been employed to determine the composi-
tion of Martian dune sand (M. G. A. Lapôtre et al. 2017b;
E. B. Rampe et al. 2018) as well as the surface composition of
Ceres (H. Kurokawa et al. 2020).

To solve the inverse problem, a set of sample models (up to
the length of the Markov chain L) is generated by the CATMIP
algorithm. The probability of each candidate model is assessed
for acceptance or rejection based on the goodness of fit
between the synthetic and model SSA spectra. Our sampling
approach is described in detail in M. G. A. Lapôtre et al.
(2017a), although for this study, we made some minor
modifications. CATMIP draws samples of the prior probability
density function (PDF) and then simulates a series of
intermediate PDFs that transition from the prior to the target
posterior PDF. The samples of each intermediate PDF, in turn,
are drawn using the Metropolis algorithm. The Metropolis
algorithm is a Markov chain (that is, a random walk) that
requires the use of a proposal PDF to produce candidate steps
for the random walk. To optimize sampling efficiency, for
each intermediate PDF, the proposal PDF for abundance
values is a Dirichlet distribution fit to the mineral abundance
samples of the previous intermediate PDF. Similarly, the
proposal PDF for grain sizes is a multivariate normal PDF with
covariance calculated from the grain size samples of the
previous intermediate PDF.
